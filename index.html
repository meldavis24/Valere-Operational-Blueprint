The Valère Practice Model
Core design principles that must survive any new site
Practice Ownership — Valère Structure
Valère by CDR Health is owned by Tina Vidal — a non-clinician owner. Clinical services are delivered by Melani Davis, MSN, APRN, FNP-C as the contracted clinical provider. This is a non-clinician-owned practice model, which has important implications for entity structure, CPOM compliance, and the delineation between business decisions (Tina) and clinical decisions (Melani). This distinction must be maintained and documented across all new sites. Valère is a 100% cash-pay practice — no insurance is billed or accepted.
Every Valère site — regardless of location, size, or market — must be built on the same clinical and operational foundation. What changes is the market context, the staffing mix, and the physical footprint. What never changes is the model.
Clinical Model
Functional medicine optimization practice. Hormone therapy, peptide therapy, cardiometabolic, longevity. Provider-led, protocol-governed, lab-anchored. Not a medspa. Not a weight loss clinic. A precision medicine practice.
Revenue Model
100% cash-pay. No insurance billed or accepted. Membership and service fee structure. Supplement revenue via Fullscript. IV therapy as premium service line. Telehealth as geographic extension. Owner: Tina Vidal (non-clinician). Clinical provider: Melani Davis, APRN (contracted).
Patient Model
Adults 35–65 seeking optimization, not disease management. Health-aware, income-stable, willing to invest in preventive care. Retention-focused — patients stay for years, not visits.
Non-Negotiables Across All Sites
Provider is an APRN with DEA registration and current CPA. All protocols follow The Valère Signal. Labs are drawn at every initiation and at defined monitoring intervals. Informed consent is documented every time. Emergency kit is stocked and inspected monthly. HIPAA-compliant platforms only. No clinical shortcuts regardless of market pressure.

Go / No-Go Framework
The decision logic before committing to a new site
GO — All of These Must Be True
  • Target market population and income thresholds met
  • APRN licensure obtainable in target state within 90 days
  • DEA registration transferable or new registration obtainable
  • Collaborative Practice Agreement physician identified and willing
  • Compounding pharmacy relationship established for target state
  • Physical space scored 70+ on site selection criteria
  • Startup capital secured for 12-month runway (see §3)
  • Staffing plan viable — at least MA and front desk identifiable
  • Competitive differentiation is clear and defensible
NO-GO — Any One of These Stops Expansion
  • APRN licensure requires >6-month timeline or no provider (APRN or physician) available to staff the site
  • Market has 3+ established, well-reviewed functional medicine competitors
  • Startup capital does not cover 12-month operating costs
  • No viable compounding pharmacy relationship in target state
  • Lease terms require personal guaranty beyond standard term
  • State has strict CPOM doctrine requiring professional ownership — MSO structure required (attorney-designed). This adds 4–8 weeks and $3K–$8K in legal fees vs. flexible states.
  • Existing Tallahassee site not yet operationally stable and profitable
The Sequencing Rule
No new physical site opens until the Tallahassee site runs 90 consecutive days with positive cash flow and operational independence (runs without Melani managing daily operations). Telehealth expansion into new states can happen independently of physical site decisions — different risk profile, lower capital requirement.

Business Entity Setup
Structure · State registration · Tax elections · Corporate practice rules
Recommended Structure
For a New Site in a New State
Valère is owned by Tina Vidal, a non-clinician. This is a significant structural advantage — Valère is not constrained by professional corporation requirements that apply to clinician-owned practices in many states. However, some states apply Corporate Practice of Medicine (CPOM) doctrine differently to non-clinician owned entities that employ or contract with licensed providers. Verify with a healthcare attorney in each target state. A Management Services Organization (MSO) structure — where a separate management entity (owned by Tina) contracts with a professional entity (owned by or contracted through Melani) — is the standard compliant structure in restrictive CPOM states and is worth establishing as the default architecture even in flexible states.
Entity Setup Checklist
  • Engage healthcare attorney in target state — confirm corporate practice of medicine rules
  • Form LLC or MSO entity in target state — confirm structure with healthcare attorney given non-clinician ownership model. In CPOM-restrictive states, establish a separate professional entity (PA or PC) under or alongside the MSO.
  • Obtain EIN (Federal Employer Identification Number) from IRS — free, online, same day
  • Open dedicated business bank account — separate from existing practice accounts
  • Elect S-Corp tax treatment if LLC (consult CPA — typically beneficial once income exceeds $50K). Tina as owner and Melani as employed/contracted provider have different compensation structures — formalize this with CPA before opening.
  • Register for state business tax obligations — varies by state
  • File DBA ("doing business as") if operating under Valère name rather than entity name
  • Obtain business liability insurance — separate from malpractice (see §2.4)
  • Register for state sales tax if selling supplements, equipment, or taxable services
  • Obtain NPI (Type 2) for the new entity — apply at nppes.cms.hhs.gov

Documents Required for Most State APRN Applications
  • Current Florida APRN license — certified copy (not a printout)
  • National certification — FNP-C certification from AANP or ANCC — current certificate
  • Nursing license from all previously licensed states — verified through nursys.com
  • Graduate transcripts — official sealed copies from University of South Alabama
  • Controlled substance history — DEA will verify independently
  • Malpractice history — completed by malpractice carrier on state form
  • Background check authorization — most states run independently through IdentoGo or similar
  • Employment/practice history — 5 years typically required, with supervisory contacts
  • Collaboration agreement — some states require before license is issued
Most Common Delay Causes
Waiting for official transcripts rather than ordering immediately · Malpractice carrier taking more than 2 weeks to complete state forms — request early and follow up · Nursys verification delays for licenses from states not on the Nursys system · Background check scheduling backlog — book immediately after submitting application. Assign a calendar block to follow up on each pending item every 2 weeks.

DEA Registration for New Sites
New location registration · Multi-state requirements · Timeline
Key Rules
  • DEA registration is site-specific: Your current Florida DEA registration is tied to 313 Norton Drive. A new physical location requires a new DEA registration or registration modification — even in the same state.
  • New state = new state DEA registration: Each state requires its own DEA registration number. You cannot use your Florida DEA number to prescribe controlled substances for patients physically in another state under most circumstances (telehealth flexibilities are the current exception — see §13 of The Valère Signal).
  • Timeline: New DEA registration typically takes 4–6 weeks for approval. Apply immediately after state APRN license is issued — DEA requires state license first.
  • Fee: DEA registration fee is currently $888 for a 3-year term. Budget accordingly for each new state/location.
  • Controlled substance inventory: Each new physical location requires its own controlled substance storage (Schedule III must be stored securely per DEA standards), logbook, and biennial inventory.
DEA New Site Application Checklist
  • Confirm state APRN license is issued and active in target state
  • Apply at DEA Diversion Control Division (deadiversion.usdoj.gov) for new registration
  • Specify new site address exactly as it will appear on prescriptions
  • Select appropriate practitioner type (mid-level practitioner in most states)
  • Submit payment ($888 for 3-year term)
  • Receive DEA registration certificate — post at registered address (required)
  • Register for state PDMP in new state if required (most states require PDMP enrollment)
  • Configure EPCS in EHR for new location DEA number
  • Establish secure storage for Schedule III controlled substances at new location

Collaborative Practice Agreement
Finding a collaborating physician · Agreement terms · Ongoing requirements
When a CPA Is Required
  • Required in Florida for all APRN prescribing regardless of experience level
  • Required in states without full independent APRN practice authority (varies — check The Valère Signal §13 for state list)
  • Not required in full-practice states (Arizona, Colorado, Montana, New Mexico) after meeting experience thresholds
  • Even in non-required states, having a collaborating physician as a clinical consultant is good practice and supports malpractice coverage
Finding a Collaborating Physician in a New State
  • Internal medicine or family medicine physicians are the most common collaborators for Melani's prescribing authority — align with functional medicine or integrative medicine interest. Note: the collaborating physician relationship is with Melani as the clinical provider, not with Tina as the practice owner.
  • Search: state medical board directory, integrative medicine networks (IFM physician member directory), local medical associations
  • Warm approach works best — email or call directly explaining the Valère model, compensation structure, and expected time commitment (typically minimal for a well-run APRN practice)
  • Paid CPA arrangements are legal and standard — typical range is $500–$2,500/month depending on state requirements and physician availability in that market
  • Telemedicine physicians can serve as collaborators in many states — they do not need to be in the same physical location
  • Ensure the collaborating physician is licensed in the target state and that their malpractice covers the supervisory relationship. Tina (as owner) and Melani (as clinical provider) should both understand the scope and terms of the agreement.
CPA — Required Contents
  • Scope of APRN practice authorized under the agreement
  • Prescribing authority — controlled and non-controlled
  • Formulary or medication categories covered
  • Consultation and referral protocols — when does the APRN escalate to the physician?
  • Chart review requirements — frequency, number of charts, documentation
  • Emergency protocols — physician availability and escalation pathway
  • Term and termination provisions — notice period required
  • Both parties' signatures, dates, and license numbers
CPA Risk Mitigation
Never open a new site that requires a CPA without the agreement signed, dated, and filed before the first patient is seen. A lapsed or unsigned CPA means every prescription written is potentially unauthorized. Build a CPA renewal calendar with 90-day advance reminders. Have a backup collaborating physician identified in case of unexpected departure — one-physician dependency is a single point of failure for the entire practice.

Break-Even Analysis
When does the site cover its own costs?
Break-Even Formula
How to Calculate Your Break-Even
Monthly Break-Even = Total Monthly Fixed Costs ÷ Gross Margin Per Visit Example — Standard Site: Fixed monthly costs: $22,000 Average revenue per patient visit: $275 Variable cost per visit (supplies, lab): $25 Gross margin per visit: $250 Break-Even visits per month: $22,000 ÷ $250 = 88 visits/month At 4 visits per patient per year: requires ~264 active patients to sustain. At 3 visits per week (conservative provider schedule): ~150 visits/month available. Break-even at 88 visits/month = 59% capacity utilization.

The Most Common Financial Mistake
Building out a full site (high fixed costs) before the patient panel can support it. Ramp by adding capacity — not front-loading it. Start lean. Add staff, space, and services as patient volume demonstrates demand. Every dollar of fixed cost added before revenue supports it extends your break-even timeline and increases your capital risk.

Lease Negotiation Guide
What to ask for · What to never sign without · Red flags
Non-Negotiable Lease Terms
  • Tenant Improvement (TI) allowance: Always negotiate. Standard is $20–$60/sq ft in most markets. Use to offset buildout costs. Get it in writing with a defined draw schedule.
  • Permitted use clause: Must explicitly include "medical office," "clinical practice," "healthcare services," and ideally specific language about IV therapy and diagnostic services. A vague "professional office" clause can create problems.
  • HVAC: Medical offices require better HVAC than standard commercial. Confirm system capacity handles clinical load. Negotiate new or upgraded HVAC as part of buildout.
  • Plumbing: Confirm existing plumbing supports clinical sink placement in exam rooms. Adding plumbing is expensive — factor into buildout estimate before signing.
  • Renewal options: 2–3 renewal options at defined rates give you stability and value. Without options, landlord can price you out at renewal.
  • Termination clause: Healthcare businesses close — ensure you have a termination right with defined notice (90–180 days) and reasonable exit fee rather than full lease liability.
  • Co-tenancy clause (if in medical building): If an anchor tenant (hospital, large practice) leaves the building, you may be able to reduce rent or exit. Worth negotiating in medical office parks.
Red Flags — Do Not Sign Without Resolution
  • Personal guaranty required beyond initial lease term — limits personal liability protection of the entity. If required, limit to initial lease term only and ensure it runs to the operating entity, not to Tina personally where possible.
  • No right to sublet or assign — critical if you sell the practice or need to bring in a partner
  • Exclusivity not granted — another hormone clinic could open in the same building
  • CAM charges uncapped — common area maintenance can inflate unpredictably; negotiate a cap
  • No force majeure provision — COVID taught every medical practice why this matters
  • Signage restrictions preventing exterior visibility — critical for foot traffic and brand awareness
Always Use a Tenant Representative Broker
Tenant rep brokers are paid by the landlord — they cost you nothing and represent your interests in negotiation. They know market rates, which landlords are flexible, and what TI is achievable. Using a tenant rep broker almost always results in better lease terms than negotiating directly. Ask for referrals from other medical practice owners in the market.

EPCS Configuration Is Critical Path
Electronic prescribing for controlled substances (EPCS) requires: (1) DEA registration for the new site/state, (2) identity proofing of the provider (typically done once but may need re-verification for new site), (3) EPCS module configured with the correct DEA number for the new location. Without EPCS, you cannot legally e-prescribe testosterone and other controlled substances in most states. Do not open a new site before EPCS is confirmed working — test with a non-urgent prescription before opening day.

A compounding pharmacy must be licensed to ship into the state where your patient is located — not just the state where you are practicing. Lab Account Setup
Quest · LabCorp · Specialty labs · In-office CLIA
Lab Setup Checklist
  • Quest Diagnostics or LabCorp account: Apply for a new provider account using new site NPI, address, and DEA number. Each physical location requires its own account in most cases.
  • Standing order account: Set up standing order capability for your most common panels (hormone panel, metabolic panel, thyroid panel, cardiometabolic panel) — saves time on each order.
  • Patient self-pay pricing: Quest and LabCorp offer patient self-pay (cash-pay) pricing — typically 30–60% below list price. Register your practice for this program so patients can use it without insurance.
  • Specialty labs: Dutch Test (hormone metabolite), Vibrant Wellness, Genova Diagnostics, or similar specialty labs — set up accounts directly with each company using provider credentials.
  • CLIA waiver (if drawing in-office): Certificate for Laboratory Improvement Amendments waiver — required if running any in-house testing (glucose, urinalysis, rapid strep, etc.). Apply through CMS. Takes 2–4 weeks. Annual fee ~$150. Not required if you send all specimens to reference labs.
  • Specimen shipping accounts: If drawing in-office and shipping specimens — FedEx or UPS Medical account for temperature-controlled specimen shipping.

Hiring Process

Where to Post Jobs
  • Indeed / LinkedIn: Highest volume for MA and front desk roles. Set up employer account with Valère branding before posting.
  • Local MA programs: Contact program directors at community colleges and technical schools in target market — externship-to-hire is an efficient pipeline.
  • State nursing board / APRN association: For provider hiring — post on state APRN association job board and IFM job board for functional medicine-aligned candidates.
  • Internal network: Best hires often come through referrals. Post in functional medicine Facebook groups, local healthcare professional networks.
Interview Process — MA and Front Desk
  • Phone screen (15 min): Confirm availability, compensation expectation, basic experience. Screen out obvious mismatches before investing time.
  • In-person or video interview (45–60 min): Walk through experience, clinical skills (MA), patient interaction style. Include a scenario question: "A patient calls and says their testosterone injection site is red and swollen. What do you do?"
  • Working interview (2–3 hours, paid): See how they interact with staff, handle the front desk, or support a clinical session. The most informative step — culture fit and practical skills reveal themselves quickly.
  • Reference check (2 professional): Always. Ask specifically about reliability, patient interaction, and how they handle stressful situations. "Would you rehire them?" is the most predictive question.
  • Offer: Verbal first, then written within 24 hours. Include start date, compensation, schedule, and brief overview of benefits.
Interview Questions That Reveal Fit
  • "Tell me about a time a patient was frustrated or upset. What happened and what did you do?" — Reveals composure and patient orientation.
  • "What does 'good patient care' look like to you in a support role?" — Reveals whether they see themselves as supporting the patient or supporting the provider.
  • "What would you do if a patient asked you a question about their lab results and you didn't know the answer?" — Should always answer: get the provider. Any other answer is a red flag.
  • "Tell me about a time you made a mistake at work and how you handled it." — Reveals self-awareness and accountability.
  • "What interests you about functional medicine or hormone optimization?" — Genuine curiosity matters. Patients feel it when staff are indifferent to the mission.

Required Documents — Every New Hire

At-Will Employment — But Document Everything
Most states are at-will — you can terminate without cause. But always document performance issues, attendance problems, policy violations, and corrective conversations in writing — even informally. "He said / she said" without documentation creates unemployment and wrongful termination exposure even in at-will states. Every verbal correction should be followed by a written summary email within 24 hours. Every formal write-up should be signed by the employee and filed.

    Telehealth State Expansion
    Lowest-cost, lowest-risk expansion path — expand virtually first
    Telehealth Expansion Logic
    Before opening any physical site in a new state, test the market with telehealth. Telehealth expansion requires: APRN license in the target state, DEA registration (or current telehealth flexibility coverage), and a compounding pharmacy licensed to ship into that state. Total capital requirement is licensing fees only — typically $2K–$5K total. Physical site expansion requires $75K–$350K. Test the market first. If telehealth volume validates demand, physical site becomes a lower-risk decision.
    Telehealth Expansion Checklist — New State
    • Apply for APRN license in target state — use standard application checklist (§2.2)
    • Confirm DEA coverage — existing DEA valid under telehealth flexibilities OR apply for state-specific DEA registration
    • Confirm compounding pharmacy has non-resident permit in target state
    • Add state endorsement to malpractice policy
    • Register for target state PDMP before any CS prescribing
    • Update website with telehealth service available in [state]
    • Add state-specific Google Ads targeting — "hormone therapy [city/state]" searches
    • Update scheduling system to accept patients from new state — confirm intake forms reference state appropriately
    • Train staff on state-specific documentation requirements (patient location documentation at each visit)

    Multi-Site Model
    What changes at site 2 and beyond · Leadership · Standardization
    What's Different at Site 2
    • You are no longer the operator of both sites. Site 2 must have a local Practice Manager who owns daily operations. Without this, quality degrades at both sites. This hire is the single most important decision in multi-site expansion.
    • Clinical quality requires a new control mechanism. You cannot be in both places. Create a monthly clinical audit process — chart review, patient outcome tracking, protocol compliance check — with a defined standard and a consequence for variance.
    • Culture replication is not automatic. The Valère Culture Standards in The Valère Signal §14.6 must be actively taught, modeled, and reinforced at the new site — they will not transfer by osmosis. Budget time for Melani to be physically present at Site 2 for the first 60 days.
    • Finance becomes more complex. Separate P&Ls by site. Shared overhead allocation (marketing, leadership time, playbook development) needs to be formalized. This requires a more sophisticated accounting setup and a CFO-level conversation with your CPA (accountant). Tina (Tina Vidal) as owner manages the financial architecture; Melani manages clinical delivery.
    • Vendor relationships should consolidate. As you add sites, renegotiate pharmacy, lab, and technology pricing based on combined volume. Multi-site practices have meaningful negotiating leverage that solo sites do not.

    Supplement Brand Opportunity
    Private label strategy · Sequencing · Distribution
    The Opportunity
    Valère dispenses supplements at scale through Fullscript. The natural extension is a private-label supplement line built on the clinical protocols in The Valère Signal — the exact formulations, forms, and doses already proven in the patient population. This is not a pivot — it's an extension of an existing revenue stream with dramatically higher margins.
    Why the Timing Works
    You have clinical proof-of-concept. You know which supplements move in your patient population. You know which forms and doses work. A private label product is more credible coming from a practicing APRN with a track record than from a brand with no clinical context.
    Minimum Viable Product
    Start with 3–5 SKUs — the supplements you recommend most. Likely: methylated B-complex, magnesium glycinate, omega-3, vitamin D3+K2, and one hormone-support formula. Private label manufacturing MOQ is typically 300–1,000 units per SKU. Budget: $15K–$40K for first run.
    Distribution Strategy
    Phase 1: sell exclusively through Valère patient encounters and Fullscript (if private label is permissible under Fullscript terms). Phase 2: Beyond Baseline subscriber direct-to-consumer. Phase 3: Amazon and independent retail if volume justifies. Do not over-distribute early — protect margin and clinical credibility.
    Sequencing — Do This in Order
    • Confirm Fullscript terms permit private label dispensing alongside Fullscript brands
    • Identify contract manufacturer with NSF certification or USP verification — quality matters more than cost for a clinical brand
    • Start with your highest-volume single-ingredient supplements where form and dose differentiation is clear
    • Register products with FDA (dietary supplement notification is not required but facility registration is)
    • Develop compliant labeling — FTC and FDA rules on supplement claims are strict. "Supports healthy hormone balance" is permissible. "Boosts testosterone" is not without clinical substantiation.
    • Launch to existing patient population first — gather testimonials and refine the product before broader distribution
    Do Not Do This Until
    The supplement brand should not be started until: Valère Tallahassee is profitable, at least one new site or telehealth state is operational and stable, and Melani has dedicated non-clinical time available to manage product development and marketing. Supplement brands built while the clinical practice is still finding its footing become a distraction that harms both. Sequence matters.
